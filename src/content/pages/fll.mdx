FIRST LEGO League is where most of us started. Students in grades 2 through 8 build a LEGO robot, program it through a season of missions, research a real problem, and explain their work to judges. The season starts at the August kickoff and ends at qualifiers in December and January.

## Explore or Challenge

FLL splits into two programs, and the right one depends on the age of your students.

Explore covers grades 2 through 4. Up to six students build a model and a simple robot with SPIKE Essential, then show it at a festival. No rankings and no eliminations.

Challenge covers grades 4 through 8, and it is a real competition. Up to ten students build and program a robot for 2.5-minute matches, complete an Innovation Project, and meet judges who score Core Values as heavily as they score the robot.

Ages vary by region, and this season FIRST is running two editions side by side: Founders Edition on SPIKE, and Future Edition on the newer Computer Science & AI kits. Ask FIRST Florida which one your local events use before you buy anything.

LEGO Education stopped selling SPIKE Prime and SPIKE Essential on June 30, 2026. Both kits stay legal through the 2027-28 season, and teams can still use EV3, NXT, and Robot Inventor, so a used kit, a school loaner, or a set already in a closet is enough.

## Inside a Challenge season

Challenge has four parts, and a qualifier scores all four equally: Core Values, Robot Design, the Innovation Project, and the Robot Game. Plenty of teams advance with a middle-of-the-pack robot and a project the students clearly own. The reverse is rarer.

### The Robot Game

Every team plays the same game on the same field. This season's game is BIOGLOW, which has 15 missions about biodiversity. A match lasts 2.5 minutes, and the robot runs on its own code with no remote control. Teams pick which missions to attempt and in what order.

Some of this year's missions ask the robot to fly a drone on a survey, spread exploding seeds, flip a rock, and sort leaves out of a nest without letting the katydid escape. There are upgrades to install too, such as a nesting canopy and a garden skylight. Touching the robot outside the launch area costs a precision token, and tokens are worth points.

Teams play three matches at a qualifier and keep their best score.

### The Innovation Project

Students find a problem that puts biodiversity at risk, research it, and design a solution. Judges look for the engineering design process: what the problem is, who it affects, what already exists, what the team made, and what testing taught them. The students get about five minutes to present, then answer questions. Adults can help with props and practice. The talking is the students' job.

### Robot Design

A separate conversation about the robot and the code. Expect judges to ask why the drive base is shaped that way, what each attachment does, how the code decides where to drive, and what the team tried that did not work. Judges score the process, so a simple robot the students can explain beats a clever one they cannot.

## The robot and the table

One SPIKE Prime set is enough for a Challenge robot. It has a hub with six ports, two large motors, a medium motor, color, distance, and force sensors, and a few hundred Technic pieces.

Most rookie robots look the same: two driven wheels, a caster or a pair of omni wheels, and a frame small enough to start inside the launch area. Teams build two to four quick-change attachments for their highest missions, then spend the rest of the season making those runs repeat. Code runs in Word Blocks or Python in the SPIKE app. In our experience, a team that drives one run fifty times beats a team with a better mechanism and no practice. A low battery also changes how far the robot rolls, so charge before every practice match.

The field is a mat about 4 feet by 8 feet with low walls, and it is the one piece of equipment worth building in September. A half field is enough to practice most missions if space is tight.

## What it costs

- FIRST registration is $285 for a team of up to ten. The BIOGLOW Challenge Set, which includes the mat, mission models, and rulebook, is $105 plus shipping.
- A SPIKE Prime set was $400 to $550 new before it was discontinued. Used kits and school loaners are the normal route for a new team now.
- Qualifier fees are set locally. The Central and West Florida region charges $100 per team this season.
- Shirts, snacks, travel, and lumber for the table make up the rest.

A first Challenge season runs $800 to $1,200 if the team buys a robot, and under $500 if it borrows one. Explore registration is $150 and includes the Explore set. Explore teams also meet less often.

## The weekly schedule

Plan on two meetings of about 3.5 hours each week. That is enough to build the field, teach the robot, run the project, and still get a practice match in. Around mid-November, as qualifiers get close, most teams add a session or move into weekends for driving practice and judging rehearsals.

Save the last two weeks for consistency. Make the runs repeat, say the presentation out loud in front of someone who has not heard it, and leave the new attachment alone.

## Dates near Brevard

The Central and West Florida region runs the qualifiers in our part of the state, and the schedule comes out in the fall. These are the 2026-27 dates posted so far:

- December 5, 2026: Lake County qualifier at Montverde Academy.
- December 12, 2026: Pinellas County qualifier at Safety Harbor Middle School.
- January 16, 2027: West Regional Championship at Shorecrest Preparatory School in St. Petersburg. Teams need to advance from a qualifier to attend.
- The Brevard qualifier at Rockledge High School ran on December 13 last season. This season's date is not on the region's schedule yet, and we will update this page when it is posted.

Event registration opens in early September and goes through the region, not FIRST. Slots are limited, so register as soon as your team is ready.

## Managing a team that lasts

- Split the jobs. One adult takes the robot, one takes forms, money, and travel. A parent who coordinates the others buys the coach a whole season.
- Hand the work to the students. Judges ask students what they built and why, and they can tell when a robot came from a parent's workbench.
- Recruit one grade younger than you need. Teams fall apart when their oldest students age out of Challenge.
- Keep one budget that covers registration, game set, kit, event fees, shirts, and travel. Update it once a month so nobody is surprised in December.
- Write a page at the end of the season: what worked, what you bought, who did what. It turns a rebuild into a repeat.

## Questions we get

<Collapse title="Do I need to know how to build a robot?" class="mb-5">
    No. A coach keeps the season moving: schedule, forms, snacks, and making
    sure the students finish something. FIRST's team meeting guide walks through
    the season week by week, and we can sit in on your first meetings. In our
    experience, teams rarely fail for lack of engineering skill. They fail
    because nobody planned the calendar.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="How many students should we have?" class="mb-5">
    Challenge allows up to ten and Explore up to six. Six to eight is a
    comfortable Challenge team: enough students to split the robot, the project,
    and the presentation, and small enough that everyone has a job. Ten works if
    an adult is organized about roles, because there is not enough robot for ten
    pairs of hands.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="What if no school will host us?" class="mb-5">
    FLL teams can be community teams. A church room, a library meeting room, a
    garage, or a scout troop works, as long as there is space for a 4-by-8 table
    and two adults at every meeting. Schools bring students and a gym, but they
    are not required.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="Do parents have to stay?" class="mb-5">
    At least two adults need to be present for the whole meeting, and every
    adult who works with the team has to pass FIRST's youth protection
    screening. In practice, one coach and one rotating parent covers it, and the
    rotation gives parents a reason to learn what the team is doing.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="When should we start?" class="mb-5">
    Spring. Registration opens months before the season and team numbers go
    quickly in growing areas. You also need time to find a kit, since SPIKE sets
    are no longer sold new. If you are reading this in the fall, you can still
    start, but expect a compressed season and lean on scrimmages for practice.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="What happens at a qualifier?" class="mb-5">
    Teams get a pit table, an opening ceremony, three robot matches, and one
    30-minute judging session. That session covers the Innovation Project, Robot
    Design, and Core Values together: the students present, then answer
    questions. Two coaches can watch, but they have to stay quiet. Bring the
    robot, spare parts, a charged battery and charger, a laptop with the code,
    your project materials, and a printed roster. Judging rooms usually have no
    internet and no projector, so keep the presentation on your own device or
    print it.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="What do the judges look for?" class="mb-5">
    FIRST publishes the rubrics, and the judges work from them. They watch how
    the team solves problems together as much as they look at the robot. A team
    where one student answers every question scores worse than a team where
    everyone has a part, even if the second team's robot scores fewer points.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="How does a team advance?" class="mb-5">
    The Champion's Award is the top judged honor at a qualifier, and it carries
    a bid to the state championship. A few other awards carry bids as well. Ask
    your tournament director for the exact list this season, because it changes
    from year to year.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="What if our robot does not work on the day?" class="mb-5">
    It happens, and it is not the end of the day. Judges score the process and
    the students' understanding, and a team that explains a failed mechanism
    often scores better than one that hides it. Bring spare parts and a second
    copy of the code, and let the students do the explaining.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="Our students will age out. What next?" class="mb-5">
    Seventh and eighth graders can move to FIRST Tech Challenge, which uses
    metal robots, Java, and a September-to-April season. We wrote a guide to
    [starting an FTC team](/ftc/), and some of the same students can carry a
    team from one program to the other.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="What happens to our team after this season?" class="mb-5">
    FIRST and LEGO Education both say a replacement program is coming in 2027,
    one from each organization. We do not know the details yet. Register with
    FIRST Florida for updates, and keep your table, your laptop, and your kit
    until the new programs publish their rules.
</Collapse>
