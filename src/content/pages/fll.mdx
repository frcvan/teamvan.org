FIRST LEGO League is where most of us started. Students in grades 2 through 8 build a LEGO robot, program it through a season of missions, research a problem, and explain their work to judges. The season runs from the August kickoff to qualifiers in December and January.

FLL splits into Explore for grades 2 through 4 and Challenge for grades 4 through 8. Explore teams build a model and a simple robot with SPIKE Essential, then show it at a festival. There are no rankings or eliminations. Challenge is the judged competition: up to ten students build and program a robot for 2.5-minute matches, complete an Innovation Project, and meet judges who score Core Values as heavily as the robot.

Ages vary by region, and this season FIRST is running two editions side by side: Founders Edition on SPIKE and Future Edition on the newer Computer Science & AI kits. Ask FIRST Florida which one local events use before buying anything.

LEGO Education stopped selling SPIKE Prime and SPIKE Essential on June 30, 2026. Both kits stay legal through the 2027-28 season, and teams can still use EV3, NXT, and Robot Inventor, so a used kit, a school loaner, or an existing set is enough.

## Why start one

Most of our team came through FLL. A first Challenge season runs $800 to $1,200, or under $500 if you borrow a kit. Our FTC guide puts a first FTC season at about $1,800, so FLL is the cheaper place to try a robotics team than FTC. The $285 registration covers up to ten students, and a kit bought this year stays legal through 2027-28.

Students also research a problem, build a presentation, and explain their robot to judges, so a team can use builders, researchers, and presenters. A school is not required, and neither is an engineering background; a church room, library, or garage with space for a 4-by-8 table works.

This is also the last season of the current program. FIRST and LEGO Education split after BIOGLOW, and both replacement programs arrive in 2027. A team that runs a season now will have an easier time choosing one of them.

## What a Challenge season looks like

Challenge has four parts, and a qualifier scores all four equally: Core Values, Robot Design, the Innovation Project, and the Robot Game. Plenty of teams advance with a middle-of-the-pack robot and a project the students clearly own.

<JudgingQuad />

### The Robot Game

Every team plays the same game on the same field, and the missions and field models change each season. This season's game is BIOGLOW, which has 15 missions about biodiversity. A match lasts 2.5 minutes, and the robot runs on its own code with no remote control. Teams pick which missions to attempt and in what order.

Some of this year's missions ask the robot to fly a drone on a survey, spread exploding seeds, flip a rock, and sort leaves out of a nest without letting the katydid escape. There are upgrades to install too, such as a nesting canopy and a garden skylight. Touching the robot outside the launch area costs a precision token, and tokens are worth points. Teams play three matches at a qualifier and keep their best score.

### The Innovation Project

Students find a problem that puts biodiversity at risk, research it, and design a solution. Judges look for the engineering design process: what the problem is, who it affects, what already exists, what the team made, and what testing taught them. The students get about five minutes to present, then answer questions. Adults can help with props and practice, but students present and answer questions.

### Robot Design

Judges hold a separate conversation about the robot and the code. Expect questions about why the drive base is shaped that way, what each attachment does, how the code decides where to drive, and what the team tried that did not work. Judges score the process, so the students need to explain what the robot does and why they built it that way.

## The robot, the field, and the week

One SPIKE Prime set is enough for a Challenge robot. It has a hub with six ports, two large motors, a medium motor, color, distance, and force sensors, and a few hundred Technic pieces.

Most rookie robots look the same: two driven wheels, a caster or a pair of omni wheels, and a frame small enough to start inside the launch area. Teams build two to four quick-change attachments for their highest missions, then spend the rest of the season making those runs repeat. Code runs in Word Blocks or Python in the SPIKE app. A low battery also changes how far the robot rolls, so charge before every practice match.

The field is a mat about 4 feet by 8 feet with low walls. Build it in September so teams can practice on the same layout as the event. A half field is enough to practice most missions if space is tight.

Plan on two meetings of about 3.5 hours each week. That is enough to build the field, teach the robot, run the project, and still get a practice match in. Around mid-November, as qualifiers get close, most teams add a session or move into weekends for driving practice and judging rehearsals. Save the last two weeks for consistency: make the runs repeat, say the presentation out loud in front of someone who has not heard it, and leave the new attachment alone.

## Dates and registration

The Central and West Florida region runs the qualifiers in our part of the state, and the schedule comes out in the fall. These are the 2026-27 dates posted so far:

- December 5, 2026: Lake County qualifier at Montverde Academy.
- December 12, 2026: Pinellas County qualifier at Safety Harbor Middle School.
- January 16, 2027: West Regional Championship at Shorecrest Preparatory School in St. Petersburg. Teams need to advance from a qualifier to attend.
- The Brevard qualifier at Rockledge High School ran on December 13 last season. This season's date is not on the region's schedule yet, and we will update this page when it is posted.

Event registration opens in early September and goes through the region, not FIRST. Slots are limited, so register as soon as your team is ready.

## What we tell new coaches

A coach does not need to know how to build a robot. The job is the schedule, forms, snacks, and making sure the students finish something. FIRST's team meeting guide walks through the season week by week, and we can sit in on your first meetings. Teams rarely fail for lack of engineering skill. They fail because nobody planned the calendar.

Split the jobs anyway. One adult takes the robot, one takes forms, money, and travel, and a parent who coordinates the others takes a lot of work off the coach. The students should do the building and the talking; judges can tell when a robot came from a parent's workbench. Recruit one grade younger than you need, because teams fall apart when their oldest students age out of Challenge. Keep one budget for registration, the game set, kit, event fees, shirts, and travel, and update it once a month. At the end of the season, write a page about what worked, what you bought, and who did what; next season starts from that page instead of memory.

Six to eight students is a comfortable Challenge team: enough to split the robot, the project, and the presentation, and small enough that everyone has a job. Ten works if an adult is organized about roles. You do not need a school. A church room, library meeting room, garage, or scout troop works if there is space for a 4-by-8 table and two adults at every meeting. Every adult who works with the team must pass FIRST's youth protection screening, and at least two must be present for the whole meeting.

Spring is the best time to start. Registration opens months before the season and team numbers go quickly in growing areas; you also need time to find a kit, since SPIKE sets are no longer sold new. If you are reading this in the fall, you can still start, but expect a compressed season.

At a qualifier, teams get a pit table, an opening ceremony, three robot matches, and a 30-minute judging session covering the Innovation Project, Robot Design, and Core Values. Two coaches can watch but must stay quiet. Bring the robot, spare parts, a charged battery and charger, a laptop with the code, project materials, and a printed roster. Judging rooms usually have no internet or projector, so keep the presentation on your own device or print it.

FIRST publishes the rubrics, and judges work from them. They watch how the team solves problems together as much as they look at the robot. A team where one student answers every question scores worse than a team where everyone has a part, even if the second team's robot scores fewer points. The Champion's Award is the top judged honor at a qualifier and carries a bid to the state championship; a few other awards carry bids too. Ask your tournament director for the list this season, because it changes.

If the robot fails on the day, judges score the process and the students' understanding. A team that explains a failed mechanism often scores better than one that hides it. Bring spare parts and a second copy of the code, and let the students do the explaining.

Seventh and eighth graders can move to FIRST Tech Challenge, which uses metal robots, Java, and a September-to-April season. Some of the same students can carry a team from one program to the other.
