FIRST Tech Challenge is the middle of the three FIRST programs. Students in grades 7 through 12 build a robot about the size of a milk crate, program it in Java, and compete in alliances of two robots. The season runs from a September kickoff to the FIRST Championship in April.

## Why start one

FTC is the middle step between FLL and FRC, and it is cheaper than FRC. A first season runs about $1,800 before travel, and most of the hardware comes back the next year. The robot starts inside an 18-inch cube, the field is 12 feet, and two coaches and one kit are enough to begin.

Students do the design, wiring, coding, and talking. That means a team can use students who like CAD, students who like wiring, and students who would rather keep the engineering notebook or run the judging interview. You do not need a school or a machine shop.

It is also a good place to keep students who liked FLL. FLL ends in eighth grade, and FTC runs grades 7 through 12 with a season that goes to April. Some of the same students can carry a team from one program to the other.

An FTC robot has to fit inside an 18-inch cube at the start of a match, then expand within the rules once it moves. Most teams build from aluminum channel and brackets with a kit from REV, goBILDA, or AndyMark, and add motors, servos, and sensors as the game demands.

The brain is a REV Control Hub, which holds the code and talks to a Driver Hub over Wi-Fi. Two students drive with gamepads: one moves the robot, one runs the mechanism. A match is 30 seconds of autonomous code, an 8-second scoring pause, then 2 minutes of driver control. Four robots play at once, two per alliance, on a 12-foot field.

Code is Java in Android Studio or OnBot Java, and Blocks works for a first season. A rookie team that builds the kit's starter bot, gets two reliable scoring actions, and writes one autonomous routine will land in the middle of most leagues.

This season's game is BIOBUZZ presented by RTX. Robots collect pollen and nectar, launch them into a hive, and place them in flowers. When a hive takes enough scoring elements it tips, which unlocks more nectar. The scoring elements are 3-inch plastic balls, so intakes and launchers do most of the design work.

## Who is on a team

An FTC team needs two background-screened adults and a group of students. FIRST recommends two lead coaches, and most teams run 8 to 12 students. Students do the design, building, wiring, coding, and talking. Adults keep the calendar, the budget, and the safety rules.

A student who likes CAD, a student who likes wiring, and someone who keeps the engineering notebook cover most of the technical work. A student also has to talk to judges, which is a scored skill here.

## The Florida season

Florida runs FTC as a set of regional leagues. Teams register with FIRST, pay a league fee, and play most of their matches against nearby teams. League meets are matches only. The league tournament adds playoffs, judging, and awards, and the top teams move on to the Florida Championship. The Space Coast League covers Brevard, Seminole, and Volusia counties, and most of its events are within an hour of Palm Bay.

The season starts with registration and ordering from May through August. Hardware is the long lead item, so order early enough to build over the summer. Kickoff is in September, when FIRST releases the game manual and field. Scrimmages in October are practice events with matches but no rankings or judging; they are the cheapest way to find out what breaks under match pressure. League meets run from November through January, the league tournament is in January or February, the Florida Championship is in March, and the FIRST Championship is in Houston in April.

These are the rest of the 2026-27 dates:

- December 19, 2026: Space Coast Meet 2 at Seminole High School in Sanford. Registration closes December 15.
- January 23, 2027: Space Coast Meet 3 at Odyssey Jr/Sr High School in Palm Bay. Registration closes January 19.
- February 13, 2027: Space Coast League Tournament at Hagerty High School in Oviedo. Matches, playoffs, and judging, with advancement to the Florida Championship.
- March 12-13, 2027: Florida Championship.
- April 28 to May 1, 2027: FIRST Championship in Houston.

Dates and venues move, so check the FIRST Florida event list before you make travel plans.

Every team gets an initial interview of at least 10 minutes with two or three judges. Teams may present for up to 5 minutes, then spend the rest answering questions. Judges read the engineering portfolio and may stop by the pit later in the day. The Inspire Award is the top judged honor and usually carries an advancement spot. Think, Connect, Innovate, Design, Motivate, and Control recognize specific work, from autonomous code to community outreach. There is also a FIRST Leadership Award for students in 10th and 11th grade. Judges only consider what the team discusses, displays, documents, or demonstrates at the event, so keep the notebook and portfolio in the pit.

A Driver Hub, battery, and cable bundle runs about $750 instead of the $350 hub. FIRST puts a first season at around $1,800 before travel, and the hardware is reusable. Year two is mostly registration, league fees, and replacement parts.

## What we tell new coaches

Most teams meet two or three times a week for 6 to 10 hours total, then add sessions in the month before the league tournament. That is more time than FLL and less than FRC. Build a practice field if you can. A half field is enough for driver practice and autonomous testing, and it lets the team test the robot under the same conditions as an event.

You do not need a school. A garage with power, a sturdy table, and room for a 12-foot field works, and many FTC teams are community teams rather than school teams. A school helps with recruiting and a gym, but a team of eight students from three different schools is normal in this program.

Two coaches are required, and one of them should be free to handle paperwork and questions while the other stays with the students. Students run the judging interview. One adult may observe silently if the event allows it.

None of you need to know Java on day one. Blocks gets a robot driving in a week, and the starter bot ships with sample code. OnBot Java is documented well enough for a student to learn from, and there are teams in every league that will share code and answer questions. Plan on a month of learning before the first meet.

Most of the robot carries over from year to year. Motors, servos, the Control Hub, structure, and wheels are reusable. Game-specific mechanisms are not, so budget for a few hundred dollars in parts each season. A 3D printer or CNC is a convenience, not a requirement; kits and hand tools cover a competitive rookie robot.

The usual rookie mistakes are starting with a custom chassis, skipping the manual, and leaving the portfolio to the last week. Build the starter bot first, read the scoring section of the manual twice, and update the portfolio after every meeting so it is finished before the tournament.

The differences from FRC are robot size, cost, and season length. FRC builds a 120-pound robot in about six weeks with a large kit of parts and a much bigger budget. FTC builds an 18-inch robot over four months with two coaches and one kit. FTC is the cheaper way to run a high school robotics team, and it is a good feeder into FRC. FLL is LEGO, grades 2 through 8, and a December qualifier. FTC is metal, Java, grades 7 through 12, and a season that runs to April. Students who liked the building in FLL usually like the freedom here, because the robot is not limited to one kit.

League meets build a ranking that carries into the league tournament. The tournament adds playoffs and judging, and the top teams and award winners advance to the Florida Championship in March. From there, teams can earn a spot at the FIRST Championship in Houston. Only events in your home region count for advancement.
