<Quip>Bigger than LEGO. Smaller than FRC.</Quip>

FIRST Tech Challenge is the middle of the three FIRST programs. Students in grades 7 through 12 build a robot about the size of a milk crate, program it in Java, and compete in alliances of two robots. The season starts with a September kickoff and ends at the FIRST Championship in April.

## Who is on a team

Two background-screened adults and a group of students. FIRST recommends two lead coaches, and most teams run 8 to 12 students. Students do the design, building, wiring, coding, and talking. Adults keep the calendar, the budget, and the safety rules.

A student who likes CAD, a student who likes wiring, and someone who keeps the engineering notebook cover most of the technical work. Someone also has to talk to judges, and that is a scored skill here.

## The robot

An FTC robot has to fit inside an 18-inch cube at the start of a match, then expand within the rules once it moves. Most teams build from aluminum channel and brackets with a kit from REV, goBILDA, or AndyMark, and add motors, servos, and sensors as the game demands.

The brain is a REV Control Hub, which holds the code and talks to a Driver Hub over Wi-Fi. Two students drive with gamepads: one moves the robot, one runs the mechanism. A match is 30 seconds of autonomous code, an 8-second scoring pause, then 2 minutes of driver control. Four robots play at once, two per alliance, on a 12-foot field.

Code is Java in Android Studio or OnBot Java, and Blocks works for a first season. A rookie team that builds the kit's starter bot, gets two reliable scoring actions, and writes one autonomous routine will land in the middle of most leagues. Teams that try to build the whole game in October usually end up with a robot that works in February.

## The 2026-27 game

This season's game is BIOBUZZ presented by RTX. Robots collect pollen and nectar, launch them into a hive, and place them in flowers. When a hive takes enough scoring elements it tips, which unlocks more nectar. The scoring elements are 3-inch plastic balls, so intakes and launchers do most of the design work.

## The Florida season

Florida runs FTC as a set of regional leagues. Teams register with FIRST, pay a league fee, and play most of their matches against nearby teams. League meets are matches only. The league tournament adds playoffs, judging, and awards, and the top teams move on to the Florida Championship.

## Judging and awards

Every team gets an initial interview of at least 10 minutes with two or three judges. Teams may present for up to 5 minutes, then spend the rest answering questions. Judges read the engineering portfolio and may stop by the pit later in the day.

The Inspire Award is the top judged honor and usually carries an advancement spot. Think, Connect, Innovate, Design, Motivate, and Control recognize specific work, from autonomous code to community outreach. There is also a FIRST Leadership Award for students in 10th and 11th grade.

Judges only consider what the team discusses, displays, documents, or demonstrates at the event. A good notebook that stays in the car does not count.

## What it costs

- FIRST season registration is $350 for the team.
- The Florida league fee is $150 to $300, set by each league and charged on top of registration.
- A REV starter kit is $695. goBILDA and AndyMark sell comparable kits at similar prices.
- A control system is $350 for a Control Hub, or about $750 for a bundle with a Driver Hub, battery, and cables.
- Laptops, tools, a game set, shirts, and travel cover the rest.

FIRST puts a first season at around $1,800 before travel, which lines up with registration, a kit, and a control system. The hardware is reusable. Year two is mostly registration, league fees, and replacement parts.

## The weekly schedule

Most teams meet two or three times a week for 6 to 10 hours total, then add sessions in the month before the league tournament. That is more than FLL and less than the six-week sprint of FRC.

Build a practice field if you can. A half field is enough for driver practice and autonomous testing, and it is the difference between a robot that works in the shop and one that works at an event.

## Questions we get

<Collapse title="Do we need a school or a workshop?" class="mb-5">
    No. A garage with power, a sturdy table, and room for a 12-foot field works,
    and many FTC teams are community teams rather than school teams. A school
    helps with recruiting and a gym, but a team of eight students from three
    different schools is normal in this program.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="How is FTC different from FRC?" class="mb-5">
    Size, money, and calendar. FRC builds a 120-pound robot in about six weeks
    with a large kit of parts and a much bigger budget. FTC builds an 18-inch
    robot over four months with two coaches and one kit. FTC is the cheaper way
    to run a high school robotics team, and it is a good feeder into FRC.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="How is it different from FLL?" class="mb-5">
    FLL is LEGO, grades 2 through 8, and a December qualifier. FTC is metal,
    Java, grades 7 through 12, and a season that runs to April. Students who
    liked the building in FLL usually like the freedom here, because the robot
    is not limited to one kit. We wrote a guide to [starting an FLL team](/fll/)
    if you are choosing between the two.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="None of us can code. Can we still do this?" class="mb-5">
    Yes. Blocks gets a robot driving in a week, and the starter bot ships with
    sample code. OnBot Java is documented well enough for a student to learn
    from, and there are teams in every league that will share code and answer
    questions. Plan on a month of learning before the first meet.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="Can we reuse parts from year to year?" class="mb-5">
    Most of the robot carries over. Motors, servos, the Control Hub, structure,
    and wheels are reusable. Game-specific mechanisms are not, so budget for a
    few hundred dollars in parts each season even after the first one.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="How many adults do we need at an event?" class="mb-5">
    Two coaches are required for the team, and one of them should be free to
    handle paperwork and questions while the other stays with the students.
    Students run the judging interview. One adult may observe silently if the
    event allows it.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="What does a rookie team usually get wrong?" class="mb-5">
    Starting with a custom chassis, skipping the manual, and leaving the
    portfolio to the last week. Build the starter bot first, read the scoring
    section of the manual twice, and update the portfolio after every meeting so
    it is finished before the tournament.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="Do we need a 3D printer or a CNC?" class="mb-5">
    No. Kits and hand tools cover a competitive rookie robot. Many teams add a
    3D printer later for brackets and mounts, but it is a convenience, not a
    requirement.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="How does advancement work?" class="mb-5">
    League meets build a ranking that carries into the league tournament. The
    tournament adds playoffs and judging, and the top teams and award winners
    advance to the Florida Championship in March. From there, teams can earn a
    spot at the FIRST Championship in Houston. Only events in your home region
    count for advancement.
</Collapse>
